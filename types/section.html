Content of type 'section'

Abbreviations: A. Books & B. Periodicals

Keys the bibliographic abbreviations on Folios 0314–0315 (pp. 295–296) to the eight classical treatises and ten nineteenth/twentieth-century mathematical journals cited throughout Altshiller-Court’s historical notes.

Harmonic Ranges, Pencils, and Complete Quadrangles

Elevates harmonic division (§§ 345–357, Folios 0185–0190, pp. 166–171) from a metric segment ratio to the fundamental projective invariant of harmonic pencils, complete quadrangles, and complete quadrilaterals (Pappus VII, Chasles).

Section A — Introductory (Directed Segments & Euler’s Identity)

Introduces signed directed segments on an oriented line (§§ 306–307, Möbius, Chasles), eliminating case-splitting between internal and external points of division.

Section A — Inverse Points

Defines inverse points \(P, P'\) with respect to a reference circle \((O, R)\) (§§ 358–362) via collinearity on a ray from \(O\) and \(OP \cdot OP' = R^2\).

Section A — Poles and Polars with Respect to a Triangle (Trilinear Polar)

Introduces projective duality relative to a reference triangle (§§ 547–557): the trilinear polar of a point and the trilinear pole of a transversal line.

Section A — Preliminaries

Notation conventions for polygons, circles, triangle cevians, and circum-/inradii (§ 1), ten foundational Euclidean ruler-and-compass constructions (§ 2) with 24 elementary construction exercises, and verbal restatement discipline (§ 3).

Section A — Preliminaries (Harmonic Division of a Segment & Apollonian Circle)

Internal and external division of a segment in a given ratio (§§ 54–56, Fig. 39), mutual symmetry and metric identities of harmonic conjugates (§§ 57–66), graphical quadratic segment construction (§ 67, Figs. 40a–40b), and 3 harmonic exercises.

Section A — Similitude

Introduces the constructive method of similitude (§§ 23–30): when all conditions save one linear dimension determine the shape (species) of a figure, a convenient similar prototype is constructed first and then scaled to satisfy the remaining boundary or metric condition (Euclid VI).

Section A — The General Quadrilateral

Opens the 1952 Second Edition’s new Chapter IV (§§ 240–250) by developing affine and metric properties valid for any planar quadrilateral \(ABCD\)—convex, re-entrant, or crossed.

Subject Index

Altshiller-Court’s four-page analytical Subject Index across Folios 0329–0332 (pp. 310–313), indexing definitions, loci, triangle centers, notable circles, named theorems, and transformations by article number (§§ 1–699).

The Simson Line (Wallace-Simson Theorems & Exercises)

Develops the complete synthetic theory of the Wallace–Simson line (§§ 282–305, Folios 0159–0169, pp. 140–150), discovered by William Wallace in 1799 and traditionally named after Robert Simson.

Theory and Applications of Circular Inversion

Develops conformal transformation by reciprocal radii—circular inversion \(\mathcal{I}(O, k)\) (§§ 518–546, Folios 0249–0262, pp. 230–243)—converting intractable circle-tangency configurations into elementary rectilinear geometry.

Blank Leaf and Dover Back Cover

Terminal archival leaves of the 334-page Dover witness (Folios 0333–0334): the blank recto leaf [314] and the unabridged 2007 Dover Publications back cover (ISBN 978-0-486-45805-2).

Historical and Bibliographical Notes (§§ 4–697)

Presents Altshiller-Court’s article-by-article historical and bibliographic commentary spanning §§ 4–697 across Folios 0315–0325 (pp. 296–306), documenting priority, independent rediscoveries, and journal problem origins.

Section B — General Method of Solution of Construction Problems

Pappus’s four-stage analytic method—Analysis, Construction, Proof, and Discussion (§ 4)—illustrated through five worked construction problems (§§ 5–9, Figs. 1–7), practical drafting and discussion guidelines (§ 10), and 14 exercises.

Section B — Homothecy

Develops central dilation or homothecy \(\mathcal{H}(S, k)\) (§§ 31–53) as a point-to-point geometric transformation preserving parallelism, angles, and ratio of lengths, culminating in the centers of similitude and circle of similitude of two circles.

Section B — Orthogonal Circles

Develops the geometry of orthogonal circles (§§ 363–372)—pairs of circles whose tangents (and radii) at their points of intersection meet at right angles.

Section B — Stewart’s Theorem

Establishes Matthew Stewart's 1746 cevian theorem (§§ 308–309, Stewart 1746), expressing the length of any segment from vertex \(A\) to line \(BC\) in terms of the side lengths and base segments.

Section B — The Circumcircle (incl. Problem of Pappus § 84)

Circumcircle envelope and datum (a, A, R) (§§ 68–72, Figs. 41–43), circumdiameter–altitude–bisector–median relations (§§ 73–78, Fig. 44), antipodal chord and pedal symmetry theorems (§§ 79–82, Fig. 45), the classical Problem of Pappus (§§ 83–84, Fig. 46), product of sides bc = 2Rh_a and abc = 4RS (§§ 85–86), and 35 circumcircle construction exercises.

Section B — The Cyclic Quadrilateral (incl. Ptolemy’s Theorem § 255 & Anticenter)

Studies quadrilaterals inscribed in a circle (§§ 251–267), uniting Ptolemy's diagonal theorems, Brahmagupta's area and orthodiagonal theorems, and Mathot’s anticenter.

Section B.a — Lemoine Geometry: Symmedians

Develops the theory of symmedians (§§ 558–571, Maurice d'Ocagne, Émile Lemoine): the reflections of the medians of a triangle across the corresponding internal angle bisectors.

List of Names

Alphabetical prosopographical index across Folios 0326–0328 (pp. 307–309) mapping every geometer named in College Geometry to page numbers in the text and historical notes.

Section B.b — Lemoine Geometry: The Lemoine Point

Establishes the concurrency of the three symmedians at the Lemoine (symmedian) point \(K = X(6)\) (§§ 572–587, Grebe 1847; Lemoine 1873), the crown jewel of late-nineteenth-century triangle geometry.

Section C — Geometric Loci

The method of intersecting geometric loci (§ 11), twelve fundamental linear and circular loci including the capable arc and Apollonian circle (Loci 1–12, Figs. 8–12), worked locus constructions (§§ 12–14, Figs. 13–16), and 27 locus and construction exercises.

Section C — Medians (Centroid, Complementary & Anticomplementary Triangles)

Midline theorem (§ 87) and concurrency and 2 : 1 trisection of the three medians at the centroid G = X(2) (§ 88, Fig. 47), inaugurating the synthetic theory of the complementary (medial) and anticomplementary triangles (§§ 87–110).

Section C — Menelaus’ Theorem

Proves Menelaus of Alexandria's Sphaerica transversal theorem and its converse (§§ 310–325), turning collinearity proofs across synthetic geometry into concise products of three signed ratios.

Section C — Other Quadrilaterals (Circumscriptible & Bicentric)

Examines quadrilaterals circumscribed about an incircle (circumscriptible) and quadrilaterals simultaneously inscribed and circumscribed (bicentric, §§ 268–281).

Section C — Poles and Polars

Presents the projective-metric duality of poles and polars with respect to a circle \((O, R)\) (§§ 373–393, Apollonius, Desargues, La Hire, Poncelet).

Section B.c — Lemoine Geometry: The Lemoine Circles

Constructs the two remarkable six-point circles discovered by Émile Lemoine in 1873 (§§ 588–596, Lemoine 1873): the first and second (cosine) Lemoine circles.

Section D — Centers of Similitude (incl. Monge’s Circle Theorem § 405)

Extends Chapter II’s centers of similitude to systems of two and three circles (§§ 394–409), culminating in Gaspard Monge's theorem on axes of similitude.

Section D — Ceva’s Theorem (incl. Gergonne § 331, Nagel § 333, Desargues § 343)

Presents Giovanni Ceva's 1678 concurrency theorem (§§ 326–344, Ceva 1678), pairing Menelaus collinearity with cevian concurrency and culminating in Desargues' two-triangle theorem.

Section D — Indirect Elements

Among the conditions which a figure to be constructed may be required to satisfy, elements may be given which do not occur directly in the figure in question. For instance, it may be required that the sum of two sides of a triangle shall have a given length, or that the difference of the base angles shall have a given magnitude, etc. In order to arrive at a method of solution of such a problem, it is necessary to introduce this “indirect element” in the analysis of the problem.

Section D.a — Tritangent Circles: Bisectors

Opens the theory of the four tritangent circles (§§ 111–116) by establishing the harmonic, metric, and circumcircle-midpoint properties of the internal and external angle bisectors of \(\triangle ABC\).

Review Exercises

Sixty-seven synthesis problems spanning Chapter I: Constructions (1–27), deductive Propositions (28–58), and Geometric Loci (59–67) across Folios 0048–0052 (pp. 29–33).

Section C — The Apollonian Circles (incl. Isodynamic Points & Lemoine Axis)

Studies the coaxal triad of Apollonian circles of a triangle (§§ 597–624), their two common isodynamic points \(W, W' = X(15), X(16)\), and their orthogonality to the circumcircle.

Section D.b — Tritangent Circles: Tritangent Centers

Establishes the concurrency of the internal and external angle bisectors at the incenter \(I = X(1)\) and the three excenters \(I_a, I_b, I_c\) (§§ 117–130), revealing their orthocentric duality with \(\triangle ABC\).

Section E — Power of a Point with Respect to a Circle

Formalizes Jakob Steiner's algebraic-geometric invariant (§§ 410–420): the power of a point \(P\) with respect to circle \((O, R)\).

Section D — Isogonal Lines (Isogonal Conjugates & Pedal Triangles)

Develops the quadratic Cremona transformation of isogonal conjugation with respect to \(\triangle ABC\) (§§ 625–648), pairing every interior/exterior point \(P\) with its reflection-in-bisectors conjugate \(P'\).

Section D.c — Tritangent Circles: Tritangent Radii (Carnot’s & Euler’s Theorems)

Derives the classic metric relations linking the inradius \(r\), exradii \(r_a, r_b, r_c\), circumradius \(R\), semiperimeter \(s\), and area \(S\) (§§ 131–155), highlighted by Carnot's and Euler's theorems.

Section F — Radical Axis of Two Circles

Constructs and characterizes the radical axis of two non-concentric circles (§§ 421–439, Gaultier 1813, Steiner): the straight-line locus of points having equal powers with respect to both circles.

Section D.d — Tritangent Circles: Points of Contact

Analyzes the twelve contact points where the incircle and three excircles touch the sidelines \(BC, CA, AB\) (§§ 156–174), expressing every tangent segment cleanly in terms of \(s, s - a, s - b, s - c\).

Section E.a — Brocard Geometry: Brocard Points

Introduces the asymmetric rotational jewel of triangle geometry (§§ 649–665): the direct and retrograde Brocard points \(\Omega, \Omega'\) and the Brocard angle \(\omega\) (Crelle 1816, Jacobi 1828, Brocard 1881).

Section G — Coaxal Circles (incl. Poncelet’s Theorems & Casey’s Theorem § 471)

Develops the theory of coaxal pencils of circles (§§ 440–490), Poncelet's limiting points, conjugate orthogonal pencils, and John Casey's generalized Ptolemy theorem (§ 471).

Section E.a — Altitudes: The Orthocenter

Proves the concurrency of the three altitudes of \(\triangle ABC\) at the orthocenter \(H = X(4)\) (§§ 175–182) by embedding \(\triangle ABC\) as the complementary triangle of its anticomplementary triangle.

Section E.b — Brocard Geometry: Brocard Circle (Brocard Triangles, Steiner & Tarry Points)

Unites \(O, K, \Omega, \Omega'\) on the seven-point Brocard circle (§§ 666–683), introducing Brocard’s first and second triangles and the Steiner and Tarry points.

Section H — Three Circles (Radical Center & Circles of Antisimilitude)

Analyzes systems of three circles (§§ 491–505): the concurrency of their three pairwise radical axes at the radical center and their orthogonal circle.

Section E.b — Altitudes: The Orthic Triangle

Investigates the orthic (pedal) triangle \(\triangle DEF\) formed by the feet of the three altitudes (§§ 183–200), uncovering its antiparallel sides, optical reflection law, and Fagnano's minimum-perimeter theorem.

Section F — Tucker Circles (incl. Taylor Circle § 689)

Generalizes the circumcircle, first and second Lemoine circles, and three-point pedal circles into the one-parameter family of Tucker circles (§§ 684–689, Robert Tucker 1883), highlighted by the Taylor circle (§ 689).

Section I — The Problem of Apollonius (Ten Problems I–X & Gergonne’s Solution)

Crowns Chapter VIII with the Problem of Apollonius (§§ 506–517): to construct a circle tangent to three given circles (or any combination of points P, lines L, and circles C).

Section E.c — Altitudes: The Euler Line

Establishes Leonhard Euler's 1765 discovery (§§ 201–205, Euler 1765) that the circumcenter \(O\), centroid \(G\), and orthocenter \(H\) of any non-equilateral triangle are collinear on the Euler line.

Section G — The Orthopole

Culminates the entire 699-article treatise with Joseph Neuberg's theory of the orthopole \(M\) of a line \(m\) with respect to \(\triangle ABC\) (§§ 690–699, Gallatly 1913).

Section F — The Nine-Point Circle (incl. Feuerbach’s Theorem § 215)

Presents two jewels of nineteenth-century synthetic geometry (§§ 206–219): the nine-point circle (Euler, Brianchon, Poncelet) and Feuerbach's theorem (§ 215, Feuerbach 1822).

Supplementary Exercises & Blank Verso

Concludes Chapter X across Folios 0310–0313 (pp. 291–[294]) with advanced supplementary problems spanning trilinear polarity, Lemoine–Brocard geometry, Tucker circles, and orthopoles, followed by the blank verso leaf [294].

Section G — The Orthocentric Quadrilateral

Reveals the symmetric structure of the orthocentric quadrilateral (or orthocentric group) \(\{A, B, C, H\}\) (§§ 220–239), in which each point is the orthocenter of the triangle formed by the other three.

Review & Miscellaneous Exercises

Concludes Chapter III across Folios 0134–0142 (pp. 115–123) with a comprehensive corpus of synthesis constructions, deductive propositions, and locus problems uniting circumcircle, centroid, tritangent, orthocentric, Euler-line, and nine-point geometry.
